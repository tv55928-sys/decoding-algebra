For teachers

Teach students to read algebra before they manipulate it.

Many errors in algebra are reading errors: a missed bracket, a sign left behind, an exponent read as a coefficient. Decoding is the habit of reading an expression's symbols, structure and meaning before doing anything to it. This app shows you how to teach that habit explicitly.

      Add-on lesson · teach with Lesson 4 (Say)

      Brackets and ambiguity

      Goal: students notice when words or symbols could mean two things, and use brackets to say exactly one. At one school, a lesson like this raised success on “add 5 to n, then multiply by 3” from 31% to 81%, and 62% still got it right a year later (). Even in Year 10, fewer than half of students in that study wrote 3(n + 5) correctly.

      1 · Everyday phrases (5 min)

      Show phrases where one word may or may not apply to everything after it. Ask: what could it mean? How would you make it clear?

      • spicy chicken and rice (is the rice spicy?)
      • small dogs and cats (are the cats small?)
      • fresh fruit and juice (is the juice fresh?)

      2 · Bridge to numbers (5 min)

      Say “twice five plus three” aloud. Pairs write every possible meaning and work each one out.

      2 × 5 + 3 = 13   |   2 × (5 + 3) = 16

      “Spoken math has no brackets unless we say them. Brackets let us write exactly one meaning.”

      3 · Practice (8 min)

      For each phrase: write both meanings with symbols, then evaluate both with the given value.

      three times x plus two (x = 4)

      3x + 2 = 14  |  3(x + 2) = 18

      the square of x plus one (x = 4)

      x² + 1 = 17  |  (x + 1)² = 25

      half of n minus 4 (n = 10)

      n/2 − 4 = 1  |  (n − 4)/2 = 3

      negative two times x minus six (x = 4)

      −2x − 6 = −14  |  −2(x − 6) = 4

      Then have students say each version aloud so a partner can write it: “3 times the quantity x plus 2”.

      4 · Two operations in order (5 min)

      “Add 5 to n, then multiply the result by 3.” Ask why 3n + 5 and n + 5 × 3 don't work, and what the brackets do in 3(n + 5).

      Exit ticket

      “Write a phrase that could mean two things. Use brackets to write each meaning.”

      Revisit

      In the study, some students later wrote correct expressions without their brackets. Return to this in warm-ups through the year.

      Model careful reading

      Say these habits aloud as you model. Precision and checking your reading against what you expect both matter for reading algebra .

      Reread. “I'll read it twice: once to find the parts, again to see how they fit.”
      Every symbol counts. “One bracket changes the meaning: 3(x + 2) and 3x + 2 are different.”
      Know your letters. “Before I start: x stands for a number.”
      Read to check. “Let me test this step by putting in a number.”
      Name it. “Not ‘this one’: the coefficient of x², which is −4.”
      Say the reason. “2(x − 3) becomes 2x − 6 because the 2 multiplies both terms in the bracket.”

      Research behind these choices

      • Concrete and abstract, both ways: tiles before symbols, and back again ().
      • Precision: each symbol says one thing: “5” is not “an odd number” (). Say “add to zero”, not “cancel”.
      • Read before writing: ask who has more and what “that” refers to; keywords are clues, not rules ().
      • Every language counts: start from students' words, gestures and drawings, then build toward the math words ().
      • Signs travel with terms: read an expression as a sum of signed parts, and name the job of each − sign: subtract, negative, or the opposite of (; ).
      • Break the left-to-right habit: put the multiplication on the left, in the middle and on the right, and have students find it before calculating (; ).
      • Plan for errors: put 2(x − 3) beside 2x − 3 and ask students to justify (; ).

      Find where it breaks down

      Newman's Error Analysis sorts errors into five stages. Work through them in order and teach to the first stage that breaks: an early error carries through everything after it. A Grade 9 case study showed how students made an error turning the words into an equation (transformation), and that early error carried through every later step (). The questions to ask are suggestions.

        The wording checks in stages 1–3 come from three places a word problem can be hard: the words, the situation, and the sentence structure. Don't simplify the wording away for students: teach them to work with it ().

        The language of algebra

        How you talk about expressions shapes how students read them. Start with your own language, then teach the vocabulary deliberately.

        Precise teacher talk

        Shortcuts like “cancel” hide the structure underneath. Rephrase them, and revoice students' informal language in the same way.

        These two ideas work together: you model the precise word, and you accept students' own words, which still carry the math, and build from them toward the precise one (; ).

        Instead of…Say…Why

        Rephrase student words

        Informal language often carries correct mathematics. In one Grade 8 class, students described a graph's scale as “I went by twos”, and the teacher built on the phrase to talk about units instead of replacing it. Name what's right, give the math phrase, then check with the student, “Is that what you meant?”, because your rephrasing can miss their idea. Then have the student use the phrase. During group work, circulate and steer the talk toward the math words: on their own, groups can reach the right answer without building the math language, and sometimes talk past each other.

        Ask when it's true

        Precision lives in the claim, not just the words. When a student makes a claim that is sometimes true, ask when it holds instead of asking for a more formal word. A precise claim can be made in everyday language.

        Student claims…Ask…Precise claim

        Unpack long phrases

        Math writing packs several operations into one long noun phrase, while symbols show each step. Teach students to unpack the phrase: find what it names, peel off each layer (“the sum of…”, “the square of…”), and build the expression from the inside out.

        PhraseLayers, inside outExpression

        Unpack in reverse too. Ask students to write a situation that matches an expression such as 6 × 2 + 5 × 3. In one study of Grades 6–8, only 24% could write a matching word problem, although 92–97% could write an expression for a word problem. Many stories didn't match the structure of the expression ().

        Signal words: from words to symbols

        Words in orange flip the order when written as symbols. Teach these as a set, with the order flips flagged, and teach them as clues to check, not rules.

        When the keyword misleads. In a word problem, a signal word can point to the wrong operation. Compare these two:

        Keyword matches

        Sam has $12. Lee has $4 more than Sam. How much does Lee have? 12 + 4

        Keyword misleads

        Sam has $12. That is $4 more than Lee has. How much does Lee have? 12 − 4

        Ask “who has more?” and “what does that refer to?” before writing symbols. Students made more errors on the second kind. For students who were otherwise good at word problems, “less than” that had to become addition was hardest ().
